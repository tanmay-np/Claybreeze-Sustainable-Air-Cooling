ClayBreeze — A Sustainable Clay-Based Air-Cooling System

Summer heat in Indian homes, classrooms and workshops, with little affordable cooling. ClayBreeze replaces the replaceable cooler pad with porous terracotta pots, kept wet by recirculating water and ventilated by two ESP32-controlled fans. The prototype is fully built and its control logic is running; performance testing is next, so no cooling results are claimed yet.

SEC 02

The problem, and what inspired us

Summer heat in Indian homes, classrooms and workshops is a real problem, and affordable cooling is limited — the Government of India's India Cooling Action Plan notes that India has one of the lowest levels of access to cooling in the world.

During a summer internship at Tinkerers' Lab, Sanjivani University, we looked at what a household, school or workshop can actually use. Air conditioners are expensive to buy and run and use refrigerants with climate impact. Pad coolers do cool, but their pads clog, degrade and must be replaced; grass pads can also grow mould, smell and become a fire risk when dry. Our answer: replace the pad with porous, unglazed terracotta pots — natural, locally made and reusable — kept wet by recirculating water, with ESP32 control of the fans and pump. Low cost, low power and no pad replacement are the design aims; testing will show how well it works.

Cooling approaches compared (summary of project-overview.md)
ApproachPotential advantagesLimitations and considerations
Air conditionersControlled cooling across a wide humidity range; can dehumidify.High purchase and running cost and high electrical demand; refrigerants with climate impact. Out of reach for many households and schools.
Honeycomb / cellulose pad coolersCheaper; large wetted area; established designs that do cool, best in dry air.Pads clog with scale and dust, degrade and need periodic replacement; plastic bodies.
Grass pad coolers (khus / wood-wool)Local, low-cost media; simple construction.Damp pads can harbour mould and bacteria, cause odour and attract insects; combustible when dry; frequent replacement.
ClayBreeze (this prototype)Terracotta pots: natural, locally made, reusable, biodegradable — no pad to replace. The ESP32 runs fans and pump only when needed.Design aims, not results: cooling, water use, power and service life have not been measured.

Inspiration: Project Vaayu

Reporting on Project Vaayu — a separate student initiative in Delhi that used clay tea cups (kulhads), scrap wood and metal, and water circulation to build cooling units for government-school classrooms — helped inspire our clay-pot approach.

Project Vaayu is not our work, and its outcomes are not evidence of how ClayBreeze performs. Our own measurements will be recorded independently.

The question we are answering

Can a low-cost, low-power cooler that uses porous terracotta pots instead of a replaceable pad — with recirculating water and automatic fan and pump control — produce a measurable reduction in air temperature under defined conditions, with practical water use and safe operation?

SEC 03

How it is meant to work

When water evaporates from a wet surface it absorbs heat. If the clay pots stay wet and air is blown across them, the air leaving the assembly can be cooler — and it is always more humid. The two fans are mounted at the rear on the extension frame: they take in room air from behind and blow it forward through and around the pots, out of the front into the room. Because the pots do not seal against each other or the frame, some air bypasses them; that has not been measured.

Why humidity matters

Direct evaporative cooling has more potential in hot, dry air. As incoming humidity rises, the possible cooling falls, and the air leaving is always more humid than the air entering. We will measure temperature and humidity together.

    SEC 08

    Test method

    Test method only. No measured cooling results are reported on this site. These are the measurements planned for the completed prototype.

    Temperature drop

    Inlet air temperature behind the fans and outlet air temperature in front of the pots, at documented positions. ΔT = inlet − outlet.

    Relative humidity

    Inlet and outlet RH measured at the same time as temperature, because RH depends on temperature.

    Airflow

    Anemometer readings at documented points, if an instrument is available. One point is not total flow.

    Water use

    Reservoir level or mass at the start and end of a timed run; leaks, splashes and refills noted separately.

    Electrical power

    Fan and pump nameplate ratings, plus measured power only where it can be done safely.

    Method. Compare a fan-only run with dry pots against a wetted-pot run, keeping fan setting, sensor positions and duration the same, and recording ambient temperature and humidity for each run. Repeat runs where possible. Raw readings go into data/test-data-template.csv (currently a blank header). Full protocol: testing-and-results.md.

    SEC 09

    Safety and maintenance

    • Secure and terminate all fan and pump cables before powered testing (a loose fan cable is visible in the earlier assembly photographs).
    • Keep the breadboard, ESP32 and relay away from water, splashes and runoff; fit an enclosure.
    • Have any mains wiring and protection checked by a qualified person; isolate power at the source before touching wiring, tubing or pots.
    • Guard the fan blades and check the frame, welds and rods hold the pots without cracking them.
    • Use clean water, drain the reservoir when idle, and clean deposits and slime regularly.

    A 3D printing: FDM — Fused Deposition Modelling (also called FFF, Fused Filament Fabrication)

    An FDM (Fused Deposition Modelling) printer melts a plastic wire called filament and lays it down line by line, layer by layer, until the part is complete.

    1. CAD modelDraw the part in CAD (Computer-Aided Design) software; flat base, gentle overhangs, clearance for fits.
    2. ExportSTL (stereolithography mesh) or 3MF (3D Manufacturing Format, which also keeps colours and plate settings).
    3. SliceBambu Studio turns the model into layers and toolpaths; preview the time and material.
    4. PrintClean the plate, load filament, start, and watch the first layer.
    5. Post-processLet it cool, flex the plate, remove supports and brim, smooth edges, test-fit.

      Key print settings, in one line each
      SettingWhat it does
      Layer heightThickness of each layer; thinner is smoother but slower.
      First-layer heightUsually equal or a little thicker, so the first layer sticks and hides small bed bumps.
      Nozzle diameterLine width: 0.4 mm standard, 0.2 mm for fine detail, 0.6–0.8 mm for speed and strength.
      Nozzle temperatureSet for the material: too cold gives weak layers, too hot gives strings.
      ExtruderGears that push filament into the hotend; "direct drive" means they sit right on the print head. The H2D has two nozzles.
      Bed and build plateA heated plate keeps the first layer down; the plate surface is chosen for the material.
      InfillThe inside fill of a part, as a percentage and a pattern (grid, gyroid…); more infill is stronger but slower.
      Perimeters (walls)The outline loops around each layer; most of a part's strength comes from them.
      Top / bottom layersSolid skins that close the part.
      SupportTemporary scaffolding printed under overhangs, removed afterwards.
      BrimExtra flat loops around the first layer that help small or tall parts stay stuck.
      RaftA throw-away base printed under the whole part; rarely needed on modern plates.
      Speed and cooling fanFaster saves time but can cost quality; the fan hardens each line — high for PLA, low for ABS/ASA.

      B From SolidWorks to Laser Cutter — exporting DXF files

      A laser cutter reads flat 2D outlines, usually as a DXF (Drawing Exchange Format) file. These eight steps take a panel from a SolidWorks part to a cut piece, using our own electronics enclosure as the worked example: 6 finger-jointed panels for a 120 × 150 × 100 mm box in 3 mm MDF. Menu paths follow the official SOLIDWORKS 2024 help pages, linked with each step and paraphrased in our own words.

      Common mistakes

      • Wrong scale — a drawing exported at sheet scale (for example 1:2) cuts half-size parts. Use 1:1 and check one known dimension in the laser software.
      • Dimensions exported as lines — dimension text and arrows are cut too. Hide dimensions and annotations, or export faces from the part.
      • Open contours — a tiny gap between two line ends means the part never drops out. Enable end point merging and look for closed shapes in the viewer.
      • Spline issues — some laser software misreads splines or cuts them as jagged segments. Export splines as polylines, or redraw curves as arcs.
      • Duplicate lines — overlapping edges (common where two panels share an edge in one file) are cut twice and char the edge. Delete the duplicates.

      Full text: solidworks-to-dxf.md. Single-panel preview generated from the DXF: enclosure-panel-150x100.svg.

      C Laser cutting (CO2)

      Practice pieces done; applied in ClayBreeze as the laser-cut 3 mm MDF enclosure for the electronics — see Applied in ClayBreeze.

      A CO2 (carbon dioxide) laser focuses an invisible infrared beam onto a sheet. It either burns a narrow line right through (cutting) or marks the surface (engraving). Lab machine: SIL CO2 laser cutter, 100 W.

      1 / 6

      How the laser head moves

      Seen from above. The laser tube stays still at the back of the machine; only mirrors move. The head travels left–right along the gantry (X) and the gantry travels front–back on two rails (Y). The beam bounces off three mirrors and is focused by a lens in the head onto the sheet. The head here traces one side panel of our enclosure.

      Cut or engrave?

      Unidirectional vs bidirectional engraving

      A raster engraving is built from many parallel lines. The machine can fire in one direction only, or on both passes.

      Kerf: why press-fits come out loose

      Dashed: the drawn path · filled: the part after cutting Orange: gap

      narrowwide

      Kerf is the width of material the beam burns away. Half of it comes off each side of the drawn line, so tabs shrink and slots grow. Compensation moves each path out (or in) by half the kerf so the parts come out at the drawn size. Measure the real kerf on a test cut; this demo is conceptual, with no real values.

      Laser settings, in one line each
      SettingWhat it does
      Power %Share of the tube's full output; more cuts deeper but chars edges and widens the kerf.
      SpeedHow fast the head moves; slower puts more energy into each millimetre.
      Frequency / PPIHow often the laser pulses (PPI: pulses per inch); lower tends to char wood less, higher gives smoother acrylic edges.
      Focus / focal lengthThe distance at which the beam is narrowest; the sheet surface must sit there.
      Kerf compensationOffsetting paths by half the kerf so press-fit parts come out at size.
      Vector vs rasterVector follows a line (cut or score); raster sweeps back and forth to engrave an area or picture.
      Air assistAn air jet at the nozzle that blows away smoke and flame and keeps the lens clean.
      PassesRepeating a path; two gentler passes can char less than one slow pass.
      Allowed
      • MDF (medium-density fibreboard)
      • Plywood
      • Acrylic (PMMA)
      • Cardboard
      • Paper
      Never cut
      • PVC / vinyl — gives off chlorine gas, toxic and corrosive
      • Polycarbonate — burns and discolours instead of cutting
      • Coated metals — a CO2 laser does not cut metal; coatings reflect or fume
      • Any sheet you cannot identify

      Glossary

      FDM / FFF
      Fused Deposition Modelling / Fused Filament Fabrication — melting plastic filament and laying it down in layers.
      Bed-slinger
      A Cartesian printer whose heatbed slides front–back (Y) while the toolhead moves left–right (X) and rises (Z). Our A1 and A1 mini.
      CoreXY
      A motion system where two fixed motors and crossed belts move the toolhead in X and Y; the bed moves down (Z). Our P1S and H2D.
      Gantry
      The beam that carries the toolhead (printer) or laser head across the machine.
      CAD
      Computer-Aided Design — software used to draw parts.
      STL
      Stereolithography file — a 3D model saved as a mesh of triangles.
      3MF
      3D Manufacturing Format — a 3D file that also keeps colours, units and plate settings.
      PLA
      Polylactic acid — the easiest everyday filament.
      PETG
      Polyethylene terephthalate glycol-modified — tougher, more heat-tolerant filament.
      ABS
      Acrylonitrile butadiene styrene — heat-resistant filament that needs an enclosure.
      ASA
      Acrylonitrile styrene acrylate — an ABS-like filament.
      TPU
      Thermoplastic polyurethane — flexible, rubber-like filament.
      AMS
      Automatic Material System — Bambu Lab's unit that holds several spools and feeds them automatically.
      Infill
      The inside fill of a printed part.
      Perimeter
      An outline loop (wall) around each layer.
      Support
      Temporary scaffolding printed under overhangs.
      Brim
      Flat loops around the first layer that help it stick.
      Raft
      A throw-away base printed under the whole part.
      DXF
      Drawing Exchange Format — a 2D CAD drawing file.
      SVG
      Scalable Vector Graphics — a 2D vector drawing file.
      CO2 laser
      Carbon dioxide laser — the infrared laser used for cutting and engraving.
      Kerf
      The width of material the laser beam burns away.
      Uni- / bidirectional
      Raster engraving that fires on one pass direction only, or on both passes.
      Scan offset
      A timing correction for bidirectional engraving, so lines engraved in opposite directions line up.
      MDF
      Medium-density fibreboard — the 3 mm sheet used for our enclosure.
      PPI
      Pulses per inch — how often the laser fires along a path.
      PPE
      Personal protective equipment — glasses, gloves and similar.
